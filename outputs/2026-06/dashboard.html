San Francisco ambulance response: June 2026

Are ambulances reaching life-threatening emergencies within 10 minutes, 90% of the time, as city policy requires? And does the number the city publishes reflect what callers actually experience?

The short answer

  1. The published number can be approximately reproduced. The city reported 88.4% for June 2026. Our closest reconstruction (clock starts when the ambulance is dispatched) gives 85.6%. Both are below the 90% target.
  2. Callers experience something worse. Counting from the moment the 911 call is received, only 67.7% got an ambulance within 10 minutes, 17.9 points lower. The published number doesn't count the time spent answering and triaging the call.
  3. The biggest delay is at the hospital, not on the road. After arriving at a hospital, an ambulance takes a median 40.7 min to get back in service, against a 30-minute standard. Compare 7.5 min driving to the scene and 2.2 min from the call to dispatch. Time past the standard cost 1,672 ambulance-hours this month (about 4.6 twelve-hour ambulance shifts a day).

6. What this page can't tell you

  • The city doesn't publish how it calculates its number. The “best fit” is our inference from the data, and no official owner has confirmed it yet.
  • “On scene” means the unit reached the address, not the patient's side.
  • Hospital time includes cleaning and restocking the ambulance, so it overstates the hospital's share.
  • The stricter 20-minute patient hand-over (offload) standard can't be measured from this data: it needs a hospital timestamp that isn't recorded here.
  • The data doesn't say which hospital or which private ambulance company was involved.
  • These figures describe what happened. They don't show why (association, not causation).
Glossary: terms used on this page
Life-threatening call
An emergency coded urgent (“Code 3”: lights and sirens).
10-minute compliance
Share of those calls where an ambulance was on scene within 10 minutes. The city's goal is 90%.
Dispatch clock vs 911-call clock
When the 10-minute timer starts: when an ambulance is sent, or when the caller's 911 call is received. The difference is the time spent answering and triaging the call.
Best fit (★)
The way of counting that comes closest to the city's published number across all months.
Median
The middle value: half of cases are faster, half slower.
1 in 10 takes longer than (p90)
90% of cases are faster than this. It describes the slow cases.
pts (percentage points)
The difference between two percentages: 85% vs 88% is 3 pts.
SFFD vs private ambulances
City-run ambulances (MEDIC) vs contracted private ones (PRIVATE).
Ambulance-hours lost
Minutes each ambulance spent at a hospital beyond the 30-minute standard, added up and shown in hours.
